Reviewing: A brief guide

The first rule of reviewing The first rule of reviewing is that there are no rules. But these observations about the different kinds of reviewing, and the different kinds of writing and thinking that go into reviews, may be helpful to you. Some reviewers use on particular approach, characteristically, if not always, but other reviewers are eclectic and make use of more than one approach in the same review.

Preparation. Read everything you have time for about the maker, the work, the context; read past reviews, interviews, news. Don't just do a web search, and if you do, don't just look at Google's top searches. Look in the article databases in your library or university, such as LexisNexis or Newspaper sources for newspaper and magazine articles, and JStor and Academic Search Premiere for scholarly articles.

Description At best, description gives the reader an idea of what it is like to be there looking at the art, watching the movie or play, listening to the music, or reading the book. At worst, description is all the reviewer offers. Description goes farther than the reviewer's evaluation in helping the reader decide whether she will like the work.

Bad descriptive writing Bad descriptive writing can have accurate observation and still be bad reviewing. Hierarchy is important — the relation between the more important and less important details. Two questions for your checklist: 1) Does the description pick out the most characteristic, significant, interesting, new or newsworthy aspect of the work? 2) Does it treat every detail as important, or subordinate the less to the more important?

Too much description can be boring; don't overwhelm the reader with detail. Good reviewing is succinct, leaves out uninteresting details, goes beyond description, and uses the more important descriptive details not for their own sake but to make an interesting point. (But if there are details that are especially interesting or fun, of course include them.)

What kind of descriptive detail? Remember that readers want informative writing. Checklist point 3) Is your review informative and factual— does it add background information and context? If not, you haven't done enough research before you started writing. Checklist 4) Is the review interpretive/evaluative? Have you made clear what you think makes the work interesting or significant? Do you place the work in the context of its medium, genre, or the artist in her milieu or relationship to other artists?

Generalizations as well as details. Good descriptive writing combines generalization with evocative detail. Consider every generalization as a claim, and make sure your descriptive details provide support for your claims. Rule of thumb (but remember that a good writer can dispense with thumbs too): For every generalization, give one or more examples.

Narrative If the work is narrative (movie, play, TV, book), the review should avoid lengthy retelling of the story. An amateurish review mostly tells the story; professional reviewing will give a capsule narrative, but then uses narrative details to illustrate and idea or support a point.

Some narration is important as a part of the description of the work, to give the reader an idea of what the experience is like. But this is different from the chronological recap of the work from start to finish, which is often found in student writing. The skilled reviewer analyzes the narration to pick out the details that are most characteristic of the work; narration is in the service of description and interpretation.

Interpretation The most interesting and ambitious reviewing focuses on ideas — and, not only in short reviews, one good idea is enough and can be used to structure the review.

Aesthetic commentary

What does the work tell us about the state of the art, what is the place of the work in contemporary artistic practice, what is the contribution of the artists to the medium or genre, what formal features characterize the work, how does it compare to other work by the same maker or other makers?  What other works is it like or unlike? Most of all, what is it about the work that accounts for the effect it has on you, that explains your experience of it, why you like it or don't, think it interesting or important or not?

Meaning and representation One of the most common approaches to narrative and other art that is not purely abstract looks for meaning in how the subject is represented. This is to be expected, because this is one of the main ways we talk among ourselves about movies, plays, TV — and  because this is rooted in the way we talk about each other. Gossip is the primordial critique, it represents and critiques representation, and it as ancient as language itself.

Much of the criticism which examines representation draws on politics, history, sociology. In particular, today's critical thinking about representation has been shaped by critical race theory, feminist and queer theory, and postcolonial theory, all of which developed in the period after the mass movements of the ’60s and ’70s began to reshape our culture. A good way to learn about these approaches is to look at the controversies and at polemical writing about controversies. As I'm writing this, people are still arguing about the 2017 Whitney Biennial and Dana Schutz's painting "Open Casket," an abstract representation of the tortured and disfigured face of Emmett Till. Because the painting drew on a lynching that was a turning point in the black freedom struggle, this controversy promises to be influential and  a point of reference in the art world and the African American cultural struggle for years. (Sources I'd especially recommend: Hannah Black's open letter calling for the destruction of the painting, Coco Fusco's "Censorship, Not the Painting, Must Go," and the Whitney Museum's panel discussion on the controversy , "Perspectives on Race and Representation: An evening with the Racial Imaginary Institute."

Popular culture is prime area for the scrutiny of representation. Gender and racial hierarchies have been a salient social and political flashpoint since the sixties, and political struggles and identity-based college studies programs have influenced successive  generations of students and critics, permeating our culture. You can find influential scholarly and alternative media especially concerned with these critiques, but you can find them also in the mainstream media, especially media with more liberal and progressive audiences.

Some of the questions you can ask about representation: What artistic choices did the artist make and what difference would different choices make to the meaning of the work? Here you can look at the choice of a subject to represent, the choice of  how the work is "framed" (what is included or left out, emphasized or marginalized), the choice of a speech or an action, a character or a relationship between characters, a plot development, a beginning or ending? Here also formalist approaches can meet critique of representation — how did the style shape the representation and were the stylistic choices themselves problematic (e.g., the choice of abstraction in "Open Casket").

Some of the more incisive critiques of representation locate the work in a broader perspective of culture, society, history, struggle. So the debate open "Open Casket" situates the painting in a history of exploitative white appropriation of black culture and history, and, also, the marginalization of people of color in the art world. The meaning of the painting is shaped not just by the intention of the artist, but by the history of the black struggle, by decades of critical race theory and by the heightened consciousness and action inspired by Black Lives Matter.

Historical, social, political context

Perspectives on representation, but also more traditional approaches bring in the political, social and historical context.  How relevant is the life of the artist or the circumstances of the artist when she created this work? Do biographical insights help the reader understand the work, or are they presented as interesting in themselves but not explanatory? Does the work reflect or comment on our culture, values, thinking? What does it reveal about how people live, and does the work, in telling its story or projecting its image, reveal deeper themes and social patterns?

Political: a subset of the sociological What is the ideological content of the work — does it support or challenge dominant values, ideas, institutions? Does it reflect on contemporary issues, and if so, from what point of view? Does it express a perspective of or take a stand for an oppressed group or for humanity? Does it express a simple or complex and event conflicted perspective?

Beware of simplification. Art and even trash are complex creations, mixing messages, values, ideas, most often not strictly consistent or coherent, and the most interesting messages maybe the ones that were unintended.

Evaluation: Show, don't tell. And don't just assert opinions Some people think reviewing is just giving personal opinions and rating the work (and some reviewing does only this). But this makes the review about the reviewer and not about the work. It assumes the reviewer is interesting and important to the reader. Unless the reader is your mother, why should she care what you like and dislike? (Well, maybe if you're Hal Foster.) Every evaluation should be supported with a clear reason, often in the form of a descriptive detail, an illustration or example.

What counts as support in a review? Can you refer to and describe some detail in the work which makes clear why you are making your evaluation or interpretation? Can you "show" as well as "tell"?

Some review media If you want to become a reviewer, read the professionals and learn from their technique. Here are some publications I especially recommend. You can also find specialized publications in art, film, literature, theater, but these publications cover all in articles written for general audiences.

  • The New York Times
  • The Guardian
  • The New Yorker
  • The New York Review of Books (for in-depth essay-reviews)
  • Hyperallergic. (The most widely read art publication at SAIC?)
