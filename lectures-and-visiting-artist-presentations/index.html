Lectures and Visiting Artist Presentations

Preparation

Where do you find the lectures to cover? Try the SAIC calendar, watch the internal emails, bulletin boards, the monitors, and, especially, the schedules for the Visiting Artists Program, Conversations at the Edge, and the Siskel Film Center, where you will sometimes find filmmakers coming as guest speakers.

Learn something about the subject or the presenter beforehand. You may find that it is hard to follow the talk without some familiarity with the subject. So, for example, if you are covering James Meyer's lecture "Maximum Impact: The Legacy of Minimalism," you might consult an encyclopedia, Grove Dictionary of Art, or an art history textbook on "minimalism."    (You could also consult the NY Times Book Review for any published author -- the book review gives free access to its entire archive). Check Flaxman's on-line resources, especially Lexis-Nexis and JStor and Academic Search Premiere and look at reviews of your subject's books or exhibitions. Look in Flaxman's art journals (indexed on-line in in the Art Index, for example) and go to the reference desk and ask for suggestions.

If you are covering a visiting artist presentation, be aware that the critics may be easier to write about than artists, who may project or broadcast samples of their work and flip through them rapidly with spontaneous comments. But if you cover an artist, you need to describe the work and quote the comments. Research beforehand helps.

At the event

Take careful notes. Use abbreviations -- develop your own "shorthand" abbreviations for frequently used words.

Be sure you get the spelling of the speaker's name right -- and the correct identification and spelling of any position or institutional affiliation, other names mentioned. If you are unsure, ask someone or get contact information so you can follow up.

In taking notes, don't get bogged down trying to write down everything. Focus on the important points -- don't write mechanically, listen carefully. Be sure to get some good quotes and concentrate on getting down a few good quotes accurately.

Remember: you need to use direct quotes as well as paraphrase and summary, but mix the two and don't use extensive direct quotation. In doing the report, you are doing story-telling -- telling the story of the event.

If you don't write fast enough, you might try recording the event. In that case, use your cell phone or use an audio recorder with a counter, set it at zero before beginning, and note the number at a moment in the talk you think you will want to quote directly or go back to. Use fresh batteries and test the recorder, and be aware the recorder might not pick up the comments clearly, or that the speaker may object to your recording them.

Be sure to cover the question and answer period as well as the talk itself.

Why not talk to the speaker after the event? Tell the speaker you’re covering the event for the school newspaper, get a few good quotes of your own, check any details you are unsure of, get contact info in case you have to follow up either from the speaker or from the event’s sponsor.

When you start writing

Crack the "nut." Be sure you have a clear "nut" -- the sentence(s) near the beginning of your article which make clear why the subject matters or is interesting to the reader.

Be sure your story has the basic information -- that you can identify the speaker by background correctly in a few sentences, the correct names of books, art works, publishers, galleries, correct dates, places, any other details.

As with any news story, you want to have some good, accurate direct quotes. You want to get quotes not just from the speaker but also from the audience in the discussion period.

Contextualize the comments of the speaker. Use the quotes you've recorded, but also your personal knowledge and background research to explain, interpret, give background, and intersperse your comments with the speaker's. Remember, you are writing a narrative, doing story-telling; story-tellers don't just narrate, they describe, they explain, they interpret. Don't write a chronological narrative of the event. Evaluate your notes, see what is most important. Remember that when you do reporting, you are telling a story; Ask, what is the best way to tell the story? Beginning with unimportant details is a mistake. You can, for example, begin with a lead summarizing what was most important in what the speaker said or the significance of the event or the nature of the controversy or subject of discussion. Or just with something that happened or was said that was interesting or unexpected or fun.

Be sure you identify the speaker, make clear why he/she is interesting or important, why the subject is interesting or important or relevant to your readers.

Avoid subjective reactions. The reader will not care if you were impressed, inspired, fascinated. The story should be about the speaker, not the reporter.

Be careful with vague descriptions and generalizations. For example, don't just say she was witty, quote a witty comment.

Editing

Be sure you go over your story before turning it in. You want to make sure that all the comments are clear, and if the quotes are not clear in themselves, that you make them clear in the context you provide. If you are very familiar with the subject matter, be sure you don't assume your readers are. Write for someone who knows nothing about the speaker.

Some examples

Sophie Lucido Johnson, "The Obsessively Beautiful Chris Ware."  F Newsmagazine, September 9, 2015.

Writer Sophie Johnson begins with a lede with an idea (his apologies) that characterizes Ware, describes his talk, gives informative details about his background, his contribution to comic art, uses quotes from his lecture with his views on comics and describes the slides he shows of the comic artists he loves, concludes with an insight into what makes Ware an exceptional artist -- his empathy.

Well-crafted newswriting in a different style: Nicole Rhoden, "An Evening with Chris Ware." F Newsmagazine, October 26, 2012. Nicole goes outside the lecture to report on Ware's background, time at SAIC, descriptions of his work and notes on a current exhibition, skillfully weaving the reporting into an acount of the lecture with quotes from Ware and gallerist Yolanda Farias.

Sophie Lucido Johnson, "Sarah Vowell: Feminist Patriot of Our Time." F Newsmagazine, November 9, 2015. 

Sophie Lucido Johnson, "A Complicated Presidency." F Newsmagazine, February 16, 2016. Michael Eric Dyson on Obama.
